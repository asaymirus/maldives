     > A Taste of the Maldives: Inside Kaagé’s Progressive Maldivian Cookbook at VARU by Atmosphere

Scraped: 2026-10-04

At Kaagé, the flavours arrive familiar, even when the presentation is not.
Tuna, coconut, chilli. Ingredients that have long defined Maldivian kitchens appear in carefully crafted dishes with a contemporary finish, yet the flavours remain rooted in something recognisably local.
That balance sits at the centre of the Kaagé Progressive Maldivian Cookbook, launched at VARU by Atmosphere on 20 August.
The cookbook takes its inspiration from Kaagé, the resort’s award-winning speciality restaurant, where traditional Maldivian recipes meet modern culinary techniques. It is an approach that does not seek to replace the flavours people know, but to find new ways of presenting them.
During the launch, that philosophy became much easier to understand around the cooking station.
Chef Niyaz took guests through an interactive masterclass built around ingredients central to Maldivian cooking: skipjack tuna, coconut and the heat of Scotch bonnet chilli.
There was an ease to the way he moved between cooking and conversation, explaining each ingredient as the dishes came together. Rather than simply showing what went into a recipe, he gave context to how those ingredients work together and the place they hold within Maldivian food.
For anyone who has grown up eating these flavours, there is something interesting about seeing them broken down this way.
Tuna and coconut can feel almost inseparable from everyday Maldivian cooking. They are familiar enough that little thought is often given to why they work so well together. Here, presented through a different culinary lens, that familiarity becomes part of the experience.
The food at Kaagé follows much the same approach. Traditional flavours remain at the centre, while technique and presentation give them a progressive expression.
It allows an international guest to encounter Maldivian cuisine in a setting very different from a local kitchen without stripping away the character that makes the food Maldivian in the first place.
The masterclass also revealed why a cookbook makes sense as an extension of the Kaagé experience.
As Chef Niyaz worked through the dishes, the conversation moved naturally between ingredients, preparation and the traditions surrounding Maldivian food. The experience became less about simply learning the steps of a recipe and more about understanding what sits behind it.
That is also where the Kaagé Progressive Maldivian Cookbook finds its purpose.
The recipes offer guests a way to recreate the flavours they encounter on the island, while the masterclass provides the context that a recipe on a page cannot always capture.
The private cooking classes are now a permanent part of the VARU by Atmosphere experience, with the cookbook available exclusively to participants. In that sense, the book becomes more than something picked up from a resort boutique. It is a memento of an experience, carrying the story of the food alongside the recipes themselves.
There is another layer to experiencing these dishes at Kaagé.
The restaurant itself is designed in the style of a traditional Maldivian house, with an airy indoor space and two courtyards. Wooden textures and floral details shape a setting intended to evoke the warmth and character of a local home.
It gives the food an appropriate setting.
Dinner here becomes a journey through Maldivian culinary heritage, drawing from the produce and seafood that have shaped island cooking for generations, from coconut and yams to tropical fruits and freshly caught fish.
That experience sits within the wider character of VARU by Atmosphere, a naturally Maldivian island escape in North Malé Atoll, 45 minutes by speedboat from Malé. Across the resort, Maldivian culture is woven into its dining, hospitality and island experiences rather than treated as something separate from them.
The cookbook feels like a natural continuation of that idea.
Maldivian food has always travelled through families, kitchens and generations. At Kaagé, those traditions are being given another route to travel.
A guest may first encounter them over dinner, then learn to prepare them alongside a Maldivian chef, before eventually carrying the cookbook thousands of kilometres home.
And somewhere beyond these islands, tuna, coconut and chilli may come together in another kitchen.
The setting will be different, but the flavours will still lead back to the Maldives.
